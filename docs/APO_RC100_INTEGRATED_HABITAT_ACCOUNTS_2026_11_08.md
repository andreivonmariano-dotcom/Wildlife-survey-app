## First-pass reviewed species accounts (100)

### Birds

#### *Aviceda jerdoni* · Jerdon's Baza

The pointed crest, densely barred pale-brown underside and broad, paddle-shaped wings distinguish this baza in flight (Bird Society of Singapore, n.d.). Jerdon’s Baza is recorded from Mindanao, including the Zamboanga Peninsula and Davao Oriental, and from Southern Leyte. Western Mindanao observations include historical reports from the Zamboanga area (Lint & Stott, 1948; GIZ, 2013; GBIF.org, 2026). Jerdon's Baza is reported from forest, stream corridors and adjoining open country, where shaded canopy gaps and streamside trees may provide concealed hunting perches. Birds wintering in Southeast Asia sometimes gather in small groups; prey is taken during short sallies from a concealed perch (Bird Society of Singapore, n.d.).

*Account references:* Bird Society of Singapore, n.d.; Lint & Stott, 1948; GIZ, 2013; GBIF.org, 2026.

#### *Butastur indicus* · Grey-faced Buzzard

A grayish face, white throat with a median dark line, and barred rufous-and-white abdomen distinguish adults (McGregor, 1909–1910). The Grey-faced Buzzard has been recorded on Mindanao, Leyte, Bohol and Siargao. On Mindanao, its localities include the Mount Kitanglad Range and Cinchona Forest Reserve in Bukidnon, Mount Tagubud in Davao de Oro, Mount Melibengoy in South Cotabato, and the Pasonanca area of the Zamboanga Peninsula. Bohol records include the Rajah Sikatuna Protected Landscape (GBIF.org, 2026; Gamalo et al., 2023; Pitogo et al., 2024). During seasonal movements, wooded ridgelines and openings along forest margins can bring passing Grey-faced Buzzards into view. McGregor reported substantial migration flocks passing the Calayan and Batan islands, with birds gathering in large numbers (McGregor, 1909–1910).

*Account references:* McGregor, 1909–1910; GBIF.org, 2026; Gamalo et al., 2023; Pitogo et al., 2024.

#### *Circus melanoleucos* · Pied Harrier

Adult male Pied Harriers have black heads and breasts, white bellies and rumps, and contrasting silvery wing panels (Bird Society of Singapore, n.d.; McGregor, 1909–1910). The Pied Harrier has been reported from Mindanao, Bohol and Leyte, including the Bukidnon and Lake Lanao areas of Mindanao. Historical field observations also documented the species on Mindanao and Leyte (GBIF.org, 2026; GIZ, 2013; Lint & Stott, 1948). The species searches open terrain in low, buoyant flight, alternating flaps and glides (Bird Society of Singapore, n.d.). Pied Harriers forage over freshwater marshes, wet grassland, agricultural fields and other open country, searching low along marsh margins and fallow fields.

*Account references:* Bird Society of Singapore, n.d.; McGregor, 1909–1910; GBIF.org, 2026; GIZ, 2013; Lint & Stott, 1948.

#### *Circus spilonotus* · Eastern Marsh Harrier

Compared with the Pied Harrier, adult males have darker streaking on the neck and breast (Bird Society of Singapore, n.d.). The Eastern Marsh Harrier has been reported from Mindanao, Siargao and Leyte. Mindanao records include the Bukidnon and Agusan Marsh areas, and a historical observation also places the species on Leyte (Lint & Stott, 1948; GBIF.org, 2026; GIZ, 2013). The Eastern Marsh Harrier hunts low over grasslands and marshes, alternating low flight with long glides (Bird Society of Singapore, n.d.).

*Account references:* Bird Society of Singapore, n.d.; Lint & Stott, 1948; GBIF.org, 2026; GIZ, 2013.

#### *Elanus caeruleus* · Black-winged Kite

Adults are pale grey above and white below, with dark shoulder patches and reddish eyes (BirdLife South Africa, n.d.). The Black-winged Kite has been documented on Mindanao, including Mount Kitanglad Range, Cinchona Forest Reserve, Agusan del Sur, and South Cotabato (GBIF.org, 2026). Hovering while searching for prey is a characteristic hunting behavior (BirdLife South Africa, n.d.). Black-winged Kites use open grassland, shrubland, cultivated land and other modified landscapes, often hunting over fields from isolated trees or poles and by hovering above the vegetation.

*Account references:* BirdLife South Africa, n.d.; GBIF.org, 2026.

#### *Haliastur indus* · Brahminy Kite

Adults have a white head and breast contrasting with rich chestnut-brown body plumage (Australian Museum, 2024). The Brahminy Kite has records from Mindanao, Samar, Leyte, Bohol, Dinagat, and Siargao, including Mount Hamiguitan Range on Mindanao and Samar Island Natural Park (GBIF.org, 2026; Senarillos et al., 2021; Pitogo et al., 2024; Gamalo et al., 2023). The Brahminy Kite occurs along coasts, especially mangroves and estuaries, and is sometimes seen over forests and inland rivers (Australian Museum, 2024). It takes fish and other animals and scavenges carrion, often snatching food from the surface in flight (Australian Museum, 2024).

*Account references:* Australian Museum, 2024; GBIF.org, 2026; Senarillos et al., 2021; Pitogo et al., 2024; Gamalo et al., 2023.

#### *Icthyophaga ichthyaetus* · Grey-headed Fish Eagle

Adults have a grey head and neck, dark brown back and wings, and a white belly and thighs. The white tail has a broad black terminal band. Immature birds are more brown and streaked, with a brownish tail bearing a darker terminal band (Forest Department Sarawak, 2026). The Grey-headed Fish Eagle has been documented on Mindanao, including Agusan Marsh Wildlife Sanctuary and Agusan del Sur (GBIF.org, 2026). Grey-headed Fish Eagles inhabit large, slow-moving rivers, reservoirs and freshwater wetlands (BirdLife International, 2024). Tall bankside trees may provide perches overlooking the water. Fish constitute much of its recorded diet, with aquatic reptiles also taken (BirdLife International, 2024). AVONET lists a culmen length of 66.1 mm, wing length of 471.2 mm, tail length of 268.8 mm, and body mass of 1590 g (Tobias et al., 2022). AVONET also reports a bill width of 16.8 mm, bill depth of 25.7 mm, and tarsus length of 81.7 mm for Icthyophaga ichthyaetus (Tobias et al., 2022). AVONET reports average beak length (tip to nares) of 36 mm, Kipp’s distance of 134.6 mm, and wing length of 471.2 mm. The corresponding hand-wing index is approximately 28.6 (Tobias et al., 2022). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Vulnerable (VU).

*Account references:* Forest Department Sarawak, 2026; GBIF.org, 2026; BirdLife International, 2024; Tobias et al., 2022.

#### *Icthyophaga leucogaster* · White-bellied Sea Eagle

Adults have a white head and underparts, grey upperwings, dark flight feathers, and a broad hooked bill (Australian Museum, 2022). The White-bellied Sea Eagle has records from Mindanao, Leyte, Bohol, and Dinagat. On Mindanao, records include Pasonanca area, Lake Holon, and South Cotabato (GBIF.org, 2026). The White-bellied Sea Eagle occurs around coastal and inland waterways, using elevated tree perches and soaring over water and adjacent land (Australian Museum, 2022). Prey includes fish, turtles, sea snakes and waterbirds; large stick nests may be reused across breeding seasons (Australian Museum, 2022).

*Account references:* Australian Museum, 2022; GBIF.org, 2026.

#### *Lophospiza trivirgata* · Crested Goshawk

A prominent crest, barred underparts and rounded wings with six visible primary fingers distinguish this forest goshawk from smaller sparrowhawks (Bird Society of Singapore, n.d.). The Crested Goshawk has been documented on Mindanao, Samar, Leyte, Bohol, and Siargao. On Mindanao, records include Mount Kitanglad Range, Mount Malindang Range, Pasonanca area, Busa Mountain Range, Lake Holon, and Surigao del Sur. On Bohol, records include Rajah Sikatuna Protected Landscape (GBIF.org, 2026). Hunts from concealed canopy or mid-storey perches, making rapid attacks on birds, lizards and large insects (Bird Society of Singapore, n.d.).

*Account references:* Bird Society of Singapore, n.d.; GBIF.org, 2026.

#### *Lophotriorchis kienerii* · Rufous-bellied Eagle

Adults show dark upperparts, a pale throat and upper breast, and chestnut-rufous belly and underwing-coverts; juveniles are paler below (Bird Society of Singapore, n.d.). The Rufous-bellied Eagle has been documented on Mindanao, Samar, Bohol, Dinagat, and Siargao. On Mindanao, records include Mount Kitanglad Range, Pasonanca area, Mount Melibengoy, Mount Tagubud, Lake Holon, and South Cotabato (GBIF.org, 2026; Pitogo et al., 2024). Wooded slopes and openings in the upper forest canopy offer views of Rufous-bellied Eagles moving above the trees. The Rufous-bellied Eagle hunts by diving from height or waiting on a perch before striking prey (Bird Society of Singapore, n.d.).

*Account references:* Bird Society of Singapore, n.d.; GBIF.org, 2026; Pitogo et al., 2024.

#### *Nisaetus cirrhatus* · Changeable Hawk-Eagle

Both pale and dark morphs occur. Broad rounded wings with approximately seven primary fingers and pronounced inward curves at the wing bases are useful in flight (Bird Society of Singapore, n.d.). The Changeable Hawk-Eagle has been documented on Mindanao, including Mount Malindang Range and Agusan Marsh Wildlife Sanctuary (GBIF.org, 2026). The Changeable Hawk-Eagle hunts from concealed perches in wooded habitats. Other listed habitat categories include wetlands, rivers, grassland and open country (Bird Society of Singapore, n.d.).

*Account references:* Bird Society of Singapore, n.d.; GBIF.org, 2026.

#### *Nisaetus pinskeri* · Pinsker's Hawk-Eagle

A crested forest eagle with feathered legs. In comparison with the North Philippine Hawk-Eagle, N. philippensis, adults are described as having a more ochraceous-tawny throat and breast and plainer brownish belly and underwing coverts. Immature birds and distant views require caution because the two species were formerly treated together (BirdLife International, 2016). The Pinsker's Hawk-Eagle has records from Mindanao, Samar, Leyte, and Bohol, including Mount Kitanglad Range on Mindanao and Eastern Samar (GBIF.org, 2026; Senarillos et al., 2021; Pitogo et al., 2024). Pinsker’s Hawk-Eagle inhabits lowland and lower-montane forest, including selectively logged or otherwise disturbed stands. It may be seen flying over open areas, but its ecological association is with forest (BirdLife International, 2024). AVONET reports average beak length (tip to nares) of 24.6 mm, Kipp’s distance of 73.1 mm, and wing length of 349 mm. The corresponding hand-wing index is approximately 20.9 (Tobias et al., 2022). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Endangered (EN).

*Account references:* BirdLife International, 2016; GBIF.org, 2026; Senarillos et al., 2021; Pitogo et al., 2024; BirdLife International, 2024; Tobias et al., 2022.

#### *Pernis ptilorhynchus* · Crested Honey Buzzard

The relatively small head, long tail, variable pale-to-dark plumage and six primary fingers in flight help distinguish this honey buzzard (Bird Society of Singapore, n.d.). The Crested Honey Buzzard has records from Mindanao, Samar, Leyte, Bohol, and Siargao. On Mindanao, records include Mount Kitanglad Range, Cinchona Forest Reserve, Pasonanca area, Busa Mountain Range, Mount Melibengoy, Lake Holon, and South Cotabato. On Bohol, records include Rajah Sikatuna Protected Landscape (GBIF.org, 2026; Pitogo et al., 2024). The Crested Honey Buzzard raids bee and wasp colonies for larvae. During seasonal movements, wooded ridgelines and canopy openings can offer views of birds crossing the forest canopy. A Taiwanese nest study also recorded frogs and lizards among food delivered to the young (Bird Society of Singapore, n.d.; Huang et al., 2004).

*Account references:* Bird Society of Singapore, n.d.; GBIF.org, 2026; Pitogo et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2004.

#### *Pernis steerei* · Philippine Honey Buzzard

Compared with the Crested Honey Buzzard, this Philippine honey-buzzard has shorter wings and a shorter, more square-ended tail. The wing edges are relatively parallel in flight, giving this species a compact, shorter-winged outline compared with P. ptilorhynchus. Its resemblance to the Philippine Hawk-Eagle can complicate distant identification (Gamauf et al., 1998). The Philippine Honey Buzzard is recorded on Mindanao, Samar, Leyte, Bohol, and Siargao, including Mount Hamiguitan Range on Mindanao and Eastern Samar (GBIF.org, 2026; Senarillos et al., 2021; Pitogo et al., 2024). The Philippine Honey Buzzard uses wooded landscapes, including upper-canopy perches and gaps between tree crowns. AVONET reports average beak length (tip to nares) of 20.9 mm, Kipp’s distance of 113.2 mm, and wing length of 378.5 mm. The corresponding hand-wing index is approximately 29.9 (Tobias et al., 2022).

*Account references:* Gamauf et al., 1998; GBIF.org, 2026; Senarillos et al., 2021; Pitogo et al., 2024; Tobias et al., 2022.

#### *Pithecophaga jefferyi* · Philippine Eagle

In flight, the Philippine Eagle shows a long tail and rounded wings (University of Michigan Museum of Zoology, n.d.). The Philippine Eagle has been documented on Mindanao and Samar. Its Mindanao records include the Mount Apo, Busa, Hamiguitan, Pasian and Melibengoy regions. A historical sighting was also reported from the Zamboanga area, without establishing current occupancy there (Lint & Stott, 1948; GBIF.org, 2026; Senarillos et al., 2021; Pitogo et al., 2024; Luczon et al., 2014). On Mindanao, Philippine Eagles nest in large trees within lowland dipterocarp and upper hill forest, and some nests occur near the transition to montane or mossy forest. Studied nest trees often stood on steep slopes and ravines (Philippine Eagle Foundation, n.d.). Tracking of six adults found varied use of forest vegetation around nesting areas (Sutton et al., 2024). A nesting study at Mount Sinaka documented a diet that included civets and Philippine colugos (Ibañez et al., 2003). An eastern Mindanao habitat model also found overlap between predicted nesting habitat and areas designated for mining or ancestral domains (Sutton et al., 2026; University of Michigan Museum of Zoology, n.d.). A genomic study compared 20 wild-captive and 15 captive-bred Philippine Eagles, finding higher genomic inbreeding among the wild-captive individuals sampled (Pascual et al., 2026). Analysis of 32 Philippine Eagles from the Greater Mindanao faunal region identified at least two maternal lineages and low mitochondrial nucleotide diversity (π = 0.00054 ± 0.00030) (Bacus et al., 2025). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Critically Endangered (CR).

*Account references:* University of Michigan Museum of Zoology, n.d.; Lint & Stott, 1948; GBIF.org, 2026; Senarillos et al., 2021; Pitogo et al., 2024; Luczon et al., 2014; Sutton et al., 2024; Ibañez et al., 2003; Sutton et al., 2026; Pascual et al., 2026; Bacus et al., 2025; Philippine Eagle Foundation, n.d..

#### *Spilornis holospilus* · Philippine Serpent Eagle

The Philippine Serpent Eagle is a medium-sized raptor with a dark brown, white-speckled head and erectile crest, yellow skin between the bill and eye, and brown plumage marked by conspicuous white spots. Dark terminal bands cross the wings and tail in flight, while pale rounded spots are prominent on the breast and wing-coverts (Department of Environment and Natural Resources, n.d.; McGregor, 1909–1910). The Philippine Serpent Eagle has been documented on Mindanao, Samar, Leyte, Bohol, Dinagat, and Siargao, including Cinchona Forest Reserve on Mindanao and Samar Island Natural Park (GBIF.org, 2026; Senarillos et al., 2021; Pitogo et al., 2024; Gamalo et al., 2023). Philippine Serpent Eagles inhabit wooded foothills, riparian corridors, forest margins and tree-covered agricultural landscapes (Department of Environment and Natural Resources, n.d.). Their calls can carry across openings in the canopy. It is a diurnal raptor often observed perched or in flight (Department of Environment and Natural Resources, n.d.).

*Account references:* Department of Environment and Natural Resources, n.d.; McGregor, 1909–1910; GBIF.org, 2026; Senarillos et al., 2021; Pitogo et al., 2024; Gamalo et al., 2023.

#### *Tachyspiza gularis* · Japanese Sparrowhawk

The adult male has a slaty upper body, chestnut-tinged breast, and a narrow dark median streak on the white throat (McGregor, 1909–1910). The Japanese Sparrowhawk has been documented on Mindanao, including Agusan Marsh Wildlife Sanctuary (GBIF.org, 2026). Japanese Sparrowhawks occur in forest and open habitats, with wooded hillsides and narrow gaps along forest margins offering possible views of birds in passage.

*Account references:* McGregor, 1909–1910; GBIF.org, 2026.

#### *Tachyspiza soloensis* · Chinese Sparrowhawk

In flight, narrow pointed wings with only four primary fingers and little underwing-covert pattern distinguish it from other sparrowhawks (Bird Society of Singapore, n.d.). The Chinese Sparrowhawk is recorded on Mindanao, Samar, Leyte, Bohol, and Siargao. On Mindanao, records include Mount Kitanglad Range, Cinchona Forest Reserve, Pasonanca area, Mount Tagubud, and Davao de Oro. On Samar, records include Eastern Samar. On Bohol, records include Rajah Sikatuna Protected Landscape (GBIF.org, 2026). Chinese Sparrowhawks occur in wooded and open habitats, and may pass over forest margins and valleys during migration. It breeds in northeastern Asia, then migrates through Southeast Asia to wintering areas including the Philippines (Bird Society of Singapore, n.d.).

*Account references:* Bird Society of Singapore, n.d.; GBIF.org, 2026.

#### *Tachyspiza virgata* · Besra

Juveniles show bold dark tail bands and a yellow orbital ring; compared with Japanese Sparrowhawk they have a proportionately longer tail and rounder wings (Bird Society of Singapore, n.d.). The Besra has records from Mindanao, Samar, Leyte, and Bohol. On Mindanao, records include Mount Kitanglad Range, Pasonanca area, Mount Hilong-Hilong, Mount Lumot, Mount Pasian, and Agusan del Norte. On Samar, records include Eastern Samar. On Bohol, records include Rajah Sikatuna Protected Landscape (GBIF.org, 2026). Besras use forest and adjoining open habitats, potentially hunting from shaded mid-canopy perches and moving through narrow gaps among trees. The species is resident or partly migratory over its Asian range; its movements vary among populations (Bird Society of Singapore, n.d.).

*Account references:* Bird Society of Singapore, n.d.; GBIF.org, 2026.

#### *Pandion haliaetus* · Osprey

A white head with a dark line running through the eye accompanies long narrow wings and dark upperparts (Bird Society of Singapore, n.d.). The Osprey has been documented on Mindanao, Samar, Leyte, and Siargao. On Mindanao, records include Pasonanca area, Mount Melibengoy, Zamboanga del Sur, and Davao del Norte. On Leyte, records include Lake Danao (GBIF.org, 2026; Pitogo et al., 2024; Gamalo et al., 2023). The Osprey captures fish by plunging feet-first into water (Bird Society of Singapore, n.d.). Ospreys use coastal shallows, rivers, reservoirs and other open-water habitats where fish are accessible.

*Account references:* Bird Society of Singapore, n.d.; GBIF.org, 2026; Pitogo et al., 2024; Gamalo et al., 2023.

#### *Anas acuta* · Northern Pintail

The breeding male combines a brown head, white neck stripe, and very elongated pointed central tail feathers (McGregor, 1909–1910). The Northern Pintail is recorded on Leyte (GBIF.org, 2026). Northern Pintails use freshwater wetlands, including shallow marshes and seasonally flooded grassland with open water and vegetated edges. A Philippine observation recorded large flocks on Manila Bay during January and February, representing wintering-season behavior (McGregor, 1909–1910).

*Account references:* McGregor, 1909–1910; GBIF.org, 2026.

#### *Anas luzonica* · Philippine Duck

The Philippine Duck is a large dabbling duck, approximately 48–58 cm long. Both sexes have a dark crown and eye stripe, a cinnamon to rufous face and neck, grey-brown body plumage, and a grey bill. A glossy green to blue speculum appears on the wing (Hong Kong Bird Watching Society, n.d.; McGregor, 1909–1910). AVONET lists a culmen length of 54.6 mm, wing length of 237 mm, tail length of 84.2 mm, and body mass of 832 g (Tobias et al., 2022). AVONET also reports a bill width of 20.1 mm, bill depth of 14.3 mm, and tarsus length of 39.2 mm for Anas luzonica (Tobias et al., 2022). The Philippine Duck has been documented on Mindanao, Samar, Leyte, and Bohol. On Mindanao, records include Mount Hamiguitan Range, Lituban–Quipit Watershed, and Mount Malindawag. On Samar, records include Samar Island Natural Park (GBIF.org, 2026). The Philippine Duck uses inland and coastal wetlands throughout its Philippine range (BirdLife International, 2016). Early observers recorded this duck in pairs on small ponds and tidal creeks, as well as locally large flocks near Masbate (McGregor, 1909–1910). Reported threats include hunting, wetland conversion and agricultural pollution (BirdLife International, 2016). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Vulnerable (VU).

*Account references:* Hong Kong Bird Watching Society, n.d.; McGregor, 1909–1910; Tobias et al., 2022; GBIF.org, 2026; BirdLife International, 2016.

#### *Anas zonorhyncha* · Eastern Spot-billed Duck

AVONET lists a culmen length of 59.9 mm, wing length of 268.2 mm, tail length of 87.2 mm, and body mass of 1156 g (Tobias et al., 2022). AVONET also reports a bill width of 20.9 mm, bill depth of 15.8 mm, and tarsus length of 39.8 mm for Anas zonorhyncha (Tobias et al., 2022). A historical spot-billed duck observation was reported from the Davao area of Mindanao under the former broad species name Anas poecilorhyncha. Its identification as the currently recognized Eastern Spot-billed Duck remains unresolved, and the report is treated as an exceptional occurrence (Hornskov, 1995; Birds of the World, n.d.). Eastern Spot-billed Ducks are reported from freshwater and coastal wetlands, where sheltered backwaters and flooded vegetation may provide places to feed and rest. The historical Davao report used an earlier, broader species name, and its modern identification remains unresolved.

*Account references:* Tobias et al., 2022; Hornskov, 1995; Birds of the World, n.d..

#### *Dendrocygna arcuata* · Wandering Whistling Duck

Chestnut wing-coverts and a warm rufous breast marked with small dark spots separate this whistling duck (McGregor, 1909–1910). The Wandering Whistling Duck has been documented on Mindanao, Samar, Leyte, Bohol, and Siargao. On Mindanao, records include Lake Lanao, Davao del Norte, and Sultan Kudarat. On Samar, records include Samar Island Natural Park. On Bohol, records include Rajah Sikatuna Protected Landscape (GBIF.org, 2026; Gamalo et al., 2023). The Wandering Whistling Duck gathers in flocks on lakes and freshwater marshes and is associated with other open wetland habitats. Historical Philippine observers recorded breeding on Siquijor, outside the Greater Mindanao islands (McGregor, 1909–1910). Introduced birds in Singapore have also mixed with Lesser Whistling Ducks (Bird Society of Singapore, n.d.).

*Account references:* McGregor, 1909–1910; GBIF.org, 2026; Gamalo et al., 2023; Bird Society of Singapore, n.d..

#### *Dendrocygna guttata* · Spotted Whistling Duck

Adults have a dark-brown crown, a narrow black stripe along the back of the neck, a grey-mottled face and whitish throat. The rufous-brown upper breast and flanks bear conspicuous pale spots, and the undertail coverts are barred black and white (McGregor, 1909–1910). The Spotted Whistling-Duck has a historical record from the Zamboanga area of western Mindanao. Its regular occurrence in the region has not been established (Lint & Stott, 1948). The Spotted Whistling Duck is reported from forest and freshwater wetlands, with a historical record from coastal marsh near Zamboanga. The coastal-marsh observation near Zamboanga is historical, with few recent regional reports. AVONET lists a culmen length of 43.7 mm, wing length of 206.6 mm, tail length of 58.6 mm, and body mass of 800 g (Tobias et al., 2022). AVONET also reports a bill width of 16.8 mm, bill depth of 14.1 mm, and tarsus length of 43.6 mm for Dendrocygna guttata (Tobias et al., 2022). AVONET reports average beak length (tip to nares) of 30.4 mm, Kipp’s distance of 55.6 mm, and wing length of 206.6 mm. The corresponding hand-wing index is approximately 26.9 (Tobias et al., 2022).

*Account references:* McGregor, 1909–1910; Lint & Stott, 1948; Tobias et al., 2022.

### Amphibians

#### *Ansonia mcgregori* · McGregor's Slender Stream Toad

A multilocus phylogeographic study detected deeply divergent Mindanao Ansonia lineages, including cryptic lineages in western Mindanao (Sanguila et al., 2011). AmphiBIO gives a compiled body-size value of 50 mm (Oliveira et al., 2017). The McGregor's Slender Stream Toad has been documented on Mindanao, including Mount Kitanglad Range, Mount Malindang Range, the Pasonanca area, Zamboanga del Norte and Zamboanga del Sur (GBIF.org, 2026; Beukema, 2011; AMNH Amphibian Species of the World, n.d.; AMNH, n.d.; Nuñeza et al., 2010). In forested mountain streams, wet rocks and nearby vegetation may offer shelter. McGregor’s Stream Toad is associated with mountain forests and streams in central and western Mindanao. Unresolved lineages within the wider Ansonia complex limit how broadly this summary can be applied (Frost, 2026). Studies of advertisement calls in the Mindanao Ansonia complex indicate geographic variation and potentially undescribed lineages (AMNH Amphibian Species of the World, n.d.; AMNH, n.d.). Compiled ecological profiles describe the species as terrestrial, aquatic and arboreal, with larval development (Oliveira et al., 2017). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Vulnerable (VU).

*Account references:* Sanguila et al., 2011; Oliveira et al., 2017; GBIF.org, 2026; Beukema, 2011; AMNH Amphibian Species of the World, n.d.; AMNH, n.d.; Nuñeza et al., 2010; Frost, 2026.

#### *Ansonia muelleri* · Müller's Slender Stream Toad

AmphiBIO gives a compiled body-size value of 38 mm (Oliveira et al., 2017). The Müller's Slender Stream Toad is recorded on Mindanao and Dinagat. On Mindanao, records include Mount Hamiguitan Range, Mount Kitanglad Range, Busa Mountain Range, Mount Hilong-Hilong, Taguibo Watershed, Mount Lumot, and Agusan del Norte (GBIF.org, 2026). Its stream-dwelling larvae require fast-flowing water, linking populations to mountain torrents and nearby forest (Sanguila et al., 2016). Mt. Hilong-hilong surveys documented specimens on forest-floor leaf litter, in rocky crevices and along stream banks between about 700 and 1,300 m (Sanguila et al., 2016). Historical and recent voucher-based records from the Taguibo Watershed in Butuan City place this toad on ground surfaces in the sampled riparian and terrestrial zones (Sanguila et al., 2021). A stomach-content study of stream-associated amphibians in northeastern Mindanao found that ants predominated in the diet of Müller’s Slender Stream Toad (Plaza et al., 2021). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Other Wildlife Species (OWS).

*Account references:* Oliveira et al., 2017; GBIF.org, 2026; Sanguila et al., 2016; Sanguila et al., 2021; Plaza et al., 2021.

#### *Pelophryne brevipes* · Western Mindanao Toadlet

A small toad with webbing on the first finger, indistinct tympanum and dark throat markings (Plaza et al., 2025). The Western Mindanao Toadlet has records from Mindanao, Samar, and Dinagat. On Mindanao, records include Mount Kitanglad Range, Busa Mountain Range, Mount Magdiwata, Mount Tubluka, Mount Sinaka, and Agusan del Sur. On Samar, records include Eastern Samar (GBIF.org, 2026; Sanguila et al., 2016). The Western Mindanao Toadlet has been observed on leaves of saplings and shrubs near streams, including forest edges and other moderately disturbed areas. Some populations remain subject to taxonomic review (Plaza et al., 2025; Sanguila et al., 2016; Oliveira et al., 2017). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Other Wildlife Species (OWS).

*Account references:* Plaza et al., 2025; GBIF.org, 2026; Sanguila et al., 2016; Oliveira et al., 2017.

#### *Pelophryne lighti* · Eastern Mindanao Toadlet

Toadlets examined from northeastern Leyte had a reported mean snout–vent length of 16.15 ± 0.62 mm (Decena et al., 2023). The Eastern Mindanao Toadlet has been documented on Mindanao, Samar, Bohol, and Leyte. On Mindanao, records include Mount Malindang Range, Mount Sinaka, Agusan Marsh Wildlife Sanctuary, and Zamboanga del Norte. On Samar, records include Samar Island Natural Park (GBIF.org, 2026; AMNH, n.d.; Diesmos et al., 2026; Frost, 2026). The Eastern Mindanao Toadlet has been observed on low ferns and shrub leaves in montane forest in the Leyte Cordillera (Decena et al., 2023). Compiled ecological profiles describe this toad as using both ground-level and arboreal habitats and undergoing larval development (Oliveira et al., 2017). It was originally described as Nectophryne lighti from the Mindanao region in 1920 (AMNH, n.d.). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Other Wildlife Species (OWS).

*Account references:* Decena et al., 2023; GBIF.org, 2026; AMNH, n.d.; Diesmos et al., 2026; Frost, 2026; Oliveira et al., 2017.

#### *Rhinella marina* · Marine Toad

Parotoid glands behind the eyes and a brown, warty back distinguish this toad (Sanguila et al., 2026). The Cane Toad is widely recorded across Mindanao, Samar, Leyte, Bohol and Dinagat. Mindanao localities include the Mount Kitanglad Range, Andanan Watershed Forest Reserve, Mount Lumot and Mount Agad-Agad, while Samar records include Samar Island Natural Park (GBIF.org, 2026; Sanguila et al., 2016; Maglangit et al., 2022; Beukema, 2011). Philippine herpetofaunal surveys document the species in human-modified environments, including agricultural and settled landscapes (Sanguila et al., 2016). At Agusan Marsh, Cane Toads were recorded around cultivated margins and freshwater-swamp vegetation (Sanguila et al., 2026). In disturbed lowland landscapes, yards, drainage edges and rain-filled pools can provide additional activity sites. In northeastern Leyte, the species was recorded in ground-level habitats (Decena et al., 2023). Compiled ecological profiles describe the Cane Toad as using terrestrial and aquatic habitats, undergoing larval development, and feeding on arthropods and some vertebrates. Activity is reported by day and night (Oliveira et al., 2017). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Other Wildlife Species (OWS).

*Account references:* Sanguila et al., 2026; GBIF.org, 2026; Sanguila et al., 2016; Maglangit et al., 2022; Beukema, 2011; Decena et al., 2023; Oliveira et al., 2017.

#### *Platymantis bayani* · Walter's Limestone Forest Frog

Adults described from eastern Samar have markedly expanded digital discs, large eyes, spotted flanks and sparse salmon-colored dorsal tubercles. Nine males measured 34.2–39.1 mm snout–vent length and nine females 44.3–49.8 mm (Siler et al., 2009). The Walter's Limestone Forest Frog has been documented on Samar, including Eastern Samar (GBIF.org, 2026; Frost, 2026). Walter’s Limestone Forest Frog inhabits the floor of limestone forest in eastern Samar (Siler et al., 2009), where shaded leaf litter and sheltered rock crevices may provide moist refuges. The name is based on an eastern Samar type series from Taft Forest at about 140 m, and was originally placed in the Platymantis guentheri group. The diagnostic identity is tied to these vouchers; superficial similarity to other local wrinkled frogs is insufficient for unvouchered records (Frost, 2026). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Vulnerable (VU).

*Account references:* Siler et al., 2009; GBIF.org, 2026; Frost, 2026.

#### *Platymantis corrugatus* · Masked Wrinkled Ground Frog

The study describes conspicuous dorsolateral folds and long dorsal ridges in frogs approximately 35–50 mm long (Plaza et al., 2025). The northeastern Leyte account describes variable brown, grey, or black facial-mask coloration and elongate tubercular ridges along the back as useful identification characters (Decena et al., 2023). The Masked Wrinkled Ground Frog has records from Mindanao, Samar, Leyte, Bohol, and Dinagat, including Mount Hamiguitan Range on Mindanao and Mount Huraw on Samar (GBIF.org, 2026; Supsup et al., 2017; Beukema, 2011). In the Taguibo Watershed survey, specimens identified as Platymantis corrugatus were recorded on ground-level habitats in the terrestrial zone away from immediate stream margins. This is a site-specific habitat-use record (Sanguila et al., 2021). In northeastern Leyte, the species was recorded in ground-level habitats (Decena et al., 2023). Mindanao populations assigned to this species complex were observed calling from exposed sites through the evening (Sanguila et al., 2016). In northeastern Leyte, males called from leaf litter and ground surfaces at night; their calls were sometimes heard from concealed sites during the day after rain (Decena et al., 2023). Compiled ecological profiles describe the species as terrestrial, and indicate direct development without a free-living larval stage (Oliveira et al., 2017). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Other Wildlife Species (OWS).

*Account references:* Plaza et al., 2025; Decena et al., 2023; GBIF.org, 2026; Supsup et al., 2017; Beukema, 2011; Sanguila et al., 2021; Sanguila et al., 2016; Oliveira et al., 2017.

#### *Platymantis guentheri* · Günther's Wrinkled Tree Frog

AmphiBIO gives a compiled body-size value of 35 mm (Oliveira et al., 2017). The Günther's Wrinkled Tree Frog has records from Mindanao, Samar, Leyte, Bohol, and Dinagat, including Mount Hamiguitan Range on Mindanao and Mount Huraw on Samar (GBIF.org, 2026; Tirona et al., 2026). In Agusan peat-swamp forest, calling frogs perched 1–3 m above the ground, while a northeastern Leyte survey recorded calling males on ferns, small branches and other low vegetation (Sanguila et al., 2026; Decena et al., 2023). Compiled ecological profiles describe the species as terrestrial and arboreal, and indicate direct development without a free-living larval stage (Oliveira et al., 2017). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Other Wildlife Species (OWS).

*Account references:* Oliveira et al., 2017; GBIF.org, 2026; Tirona et al., 2026; Sanguila et al., 2026; Decena et al., 2023.

#### *Platymantis habagatan* · Mindanao Variable Forest Frog

The 2026 original species treatment diagnoses a moderate-sized, broad-headed, terrestrial forest frog (reported specimen snout–vent length 26.7–39.9 mm; mean 33.9 mm), with unexpanded finger and toe tips, conspicuously raised subarticular tubercles, and rugose dorsal skin bearing rounded tubercles or short, irregular ridges. There may be up to six longitudinal or oblique dorsal ridges, although some specimens lack them. Background color varies from chestnut to dull brown, sometimes with thick dorsolateral markings. Compared with its sister species P. jagorii, the published sample is generally larger and has different proportional measurements and advertisement calls; compared with co-occurring P. laticeps it is smaller, with longer dorsal ridges more often present. These characters overlap among individuals. The 2026 diagnosis combines body measurements, morphology and advertisement calls to distinguish this forest frog. The Mindanao Variable Forest Frog is recognized from Mindanao, including the Mount Hilong-Hilong area of Agusan del Norte. Some older regional records were published under the broader name Platymantis dorsalis (Pitogo et al., 2026; Frost, 2026). The Mindanao Variable Forest Frog has been recorded on the forest floor of mature forest on Mount Hilong-hilong (Frost, 2026). Platymantis habagatan was described in 2026 from the southern Philippine forest-frog complex using comparative morphology, advertisement calls and genetic evidence. Earlier regional literature included these frogs under the broader P. dorsalis name (Pitogo et al., 2026).

*Account references:* Pitogo et al., 2026; Frost, 2026.

#### *Platymantis jagorii* · Jagor's Variable Forest Frog

In the 2026 redescription, this small to moderate ground-dwelling frog has a broad head, unexpanded terminal discs, prominent subarticular tubercles, a rugose back with scattered raised tubercles and as many as ten irregular dorsal ridges, and barred limbs. The examined specimens measured 23.3–39.8 mm in snout–vent length (mean 29.5 mm), overlapping substantially with P. habagatan. Its most useful reported comparative feature is its advertisement call: a long, gradually intensifying bout lasting about 60–65 seconds, composed of rapidly delivered, short clicking notes (approximately eight notes per second). Its sister species P. habagatan has markedly different call pacing and average body proportions; P. laticeps gives shorter, distinctly pulsed calls. The 2026 revision treats mainland Mindanao occurrence of P. jagorii as possible, with voucher-supported records centered on other southern Philippine islands. Jagor’s Variable Forest Frog is documented from Samar, Leyte, Bohol and Siargao. Northeastern Mindanao has also been suggested as a possible occurrence, but remains unconfirmed (Pitogo et al., 2026; Frost, 2026). Jagor’s Variable Forest Frog inhabits terrestrial forest in the southern Philippines, where shaded ground cover and deep leaf litter may offer shelter. Historical records under P. dorsalis include frogs now recognized in several related species (Pitogo et al., 2026). A 2026 revision reinstated Platymantis jagorii as a distinct species within the southern Philippine forest-frog complex, drawing on morphology, advertisement calls and molecular data (Pitogo et al., 2026).

*Account references:* Pitogo et al., 2026; Frost, 2026.

#### *Platymantis laticeps* · Broad-headed Variable Forest Frog

A relatively large, broad-headed terrestrial forest frog with rough, strongly tuberculate dorsal skin, unexpanded finger and toe tips, prominent subarticular tubercles and no long longitudinal dorsal ridges in the 2026 diagnosis. Reported adult snout–vent lengths are 34.5–45.1 mm in males (mean 39.4 mm) and 42.1–51.8 mm in females (mean 47.1 mm). The back is generally dull brown to dark brown, sometimes with lighter spots; the limbs have transverse bars. In sympatric comparisons, P. laticeps generally exceeds P. habagatan in size and head proportions and has a smaller relative tympanum. The advertisement call comprises brief bouts, approximately 2–6 seconds, with short two- to three-pulsed frequency-modulated notes, unlike the prolonged rapid notes of P. jagorii. Identification requires a combination of morphology and calling characteristics, not size or dorsal color alone. The Broad-headed Variable Forest Frog is documented in northeastern Mindanao, including the Mount Hilong-Hilong, Mount Balatukan, Mount Lumot and Mount Magdiwata landscapes of Caraga and Northern Mindanao (Pitogo et al., 2026; Frost, 2026). The Broad-headed Variable Forest Frog inhabits terrestrial forest in northeastern Mindanao, where fallen wood and leaf litter may provide sheltered ground-level spaces. Its recognition from formerly broad P. dorsalis identifications affects the interpretation of older habitat records (Pitogo et al., 2026). Platymantis laticeps was reinstated in the 2026 revision of southern Philippine forest frogs formerly classified within P. dorsalis. The study distinguished it using morphology, calls and genetic data (Pitogo et al., 2026).

*Account references:* Pitogo et al., 2026; Frost, 2026.

#### *Platymantis navjoti* · Navjot Sodhi's Cloud Frog

A small cloud frog with a smooth, finely granular dorsum that may show iridescent emerald-green, orange or creamy-yellow coloration. Its digits have moderately expanded tips but lack interdigital webbing. Male advertisement calls consist of a series of roughly 10–16 short chirping notes (Diesmos et al., 2020). The Navjot Sodhi's Cloud Frog has been documented on Leyte and Samar. On Leyte, records include Southern Leyte (GBIF.org, 2026; Frost, 2026). Navjot Sodhi’s Cloud Frog inhabits montane forest on Leyte and is also recorded from northern Leyte and north-central Samar (Diesmos et al., 2020; Frost, 2026). Cool, moist forest vegetation may offer sheltered activity sites after rainfall. Its listed Philippine DENR category is Other Wildlife Species (OWS).

*Account references:* Diesmos et al., 2020; GBIF.org, 2026; Frost, 2026.

#### *Platymantis rabori* · Rabor's Horned Tree Frog

Notable identification characters include a relatively large body and broadly expanded terminal finger discs (Sanguila et al., 2016). The Rabor's Horned Tree Frog is recorded on Mindanao, Samar, Bohol, Dinagat, and Leyte, including Mount Hamiguitan Range on Mindanao and Samar Island Natural Park (GBIF.org, 2026; Tirona et al., 2026; Diesmos et al., 2026; Sanguila et al., 2016; AMNH, n.d.). Rabor’s Horned Tree Frog often calls high in the forest canopy, producing slowly repeated pulsed notes; Taguibo Watershed surveys also document it on vegetation (Sanguila et al., 2016; Sanguila et al., 2021). Compiled profiles describe activity on the ground and in vegetation, with froglets developing directly inside terrestrial eggs (Oliveira et al., 2017). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Vulnerable (VU).

*Account references:* Sanguila et al., 2016; GBIF.org, 2026; Tirona et al., 2026; Diesmos et al., 2026; AMNH, n.d.; Sanguila et al., 2021; Oliveira et al., 2017.

#### *Fejervarya moodiei* · Asian Mangrove Frog

The Hijo Forest account describes dark markings on the upper surfaces of both forelimbs and hindlimbs and skin folds that are not arranged in regular parallel series; the features are part of a multi-character identification (Gamalo et al., 2023). The Asian Mangrove Frog has been documented on Mindanao, Bohol, and Dinagat. On Mindanao, records include Andanan Watershed Forest Reserve. On Bohol, records include Rajah Sikatuna Protected Landscape (GBIF.org, 2026). On northeastern Leyte, Asian Mangrove Frogs have been recorded in terrestrial and aquatic habitats (Decena et al., 2023). Shallow pools and muddy backwaters along mangrove margins, including brackish edges, may provide further activity sites. At Hijo Forest, these frogs were frequently found in water, particularly during wet weather (Gamalo et al., 2023). Compiled ecological profiles also describe aquatic habitat use and larval development (Oliveira et al., 2017). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Other Wildlife Species (OWS).

*Account references:* Gamalo et al., 2023; GBIF.org, 2026; Decena et al., 2023; Oliveira et al., 2017.

#### *Fejervarya vittigera* · Philippine Field Frog

This frog has an olive-grey back, sometimes marked by a pale vertebral stripe, and yellow bars on the inner hind limbs or groin. In the 2026 comparison with F. moodiei, examined F. vittigera lacked the outer tarsal or fifth-toe flap. AmphiBIO gives a compiled body-size value of 80.6 mm (Sanguila et al., 2026; Oliveira et al., 2017). The Philippine Field Frog is recorded on Mindanao, Samar, Leyte, Bohol, and Dinagat. On Mindanao, records include Andanan Watershed Forest Reserve, Cabadbaran–Santiago forest, Taguibo Watershed, Mount Agad-Agad, and Agusan del Norte. On Samar, records include Mount Huraw. On Bohol, records include Rajah Sikatuna Protected Landscape (GBIF.org, 2026; Maglangit et al., 2022). Philippine records place this frog in low-elevation ponds, flooded rice fields, irrigation channels and streamside pools, including at Agusan Marsh. Taguibo Watershed surveys recorded aquatic use, while northeastern Leyte inventories included terrestrial and aquatic encounters. Swampy forest patches have also been reported as habitat. At Hijo Forest, survey teams encountered the species frequently in swampy portions of the forest patch (American Museum of Natural History, n.d.; Sanguila et al., 2026; Sanguila et al., 2021; Decena et al., 2023; Gamalo et al., 2023). In northeastern Leyte, observers described loud, honking breeding calls and choruses that formed during or shortly after heavy rain (Decena et al., 2023). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Other Wildlife Species (OWS).

*Account references:* Sanguila et al., 2026; Oliveira et al., 2017; GBIF.org, 2026; Maglangit et al., 2022; American Museum of Natural History, n.d.; Sanguila et al., 2021; Decena et al., 2023; Gamalo et al., 2023.

#### *Hoplobatrachus chinensis* · East Asian Bullfrog

In the Asian bullfrog complex this is a robust, heavily wrinkled species with prominent dorsal ridges, dark blotches and extensively webbed hind feet; identification in Philippine invaded habitats must also consider similarly named historic H. rugulosus records and the currently accepted species concept (Frost, 2026). The East Asian Bullfrog has been documented on Mindanao and Bohol. On Mindanao, records include Andanan Watershed Forest Reserve, Agusan Marsh Wildlife Sanctuary, and Agusan del Sur (GBIF.org, 2026). The East Asian Bullfrog is reported from cultivated landscapes, where flooded ground, large pools and wetland margins may provide shallow-water habitat. Its listed Philippine DENR category is Other Wildlife Species (OWS).

*Account references:* Frost, 2026; GBIF.org, 2026.

#### *Limnonectes diuatus* · White-spined Fanged Frog

Specimens discussed from Mt. Kitanglad had a stocky body, short limbs, pale-tipped dorsolateral tubercles, and narrow light bars across the lower jaw (Beukema, 2011). The White-spined Fanged Frog has records from Mindanao and Dinagat. On Mindanao, records include Mount Hamiguitan Range, Mount Kitanglad Range, Mount Balatukan, Mount Hilong-Hilong, Taguibo Watershed, Mount Lumot, and Agusan del Norte (GBIF.org, 2026; Supsup et al., 2017; Beukema, 2011; Sanguila et al., 2021). The species has been reported from higher-elevation riparian forest in Mindanao, including mountain streams above 900 m (Frost, 2026). The Taguibo analysis assigned historical Limnonectes diuatus records to aquatic habitats (Sanguila et al., 2021). Two individuals were observed by day beneath leaf litter and rocks in a steep rocky stream at the lower Mt. Kitanglad study site (Beukema, 2011). Compiled ecological profiles describe the species as terrestrial and aquatic, with larval development (Oliveira et al., 2017). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Vulnerable (VU).

*Account references:* Beukema, 2011; GBIF.org, 2026; Supsup et al., 2017; Sanguila et al., 2021; Frost, 2026; Oliveira et al., 2017.

#### *Limnonectes leytensis* · Philippine Swamp Frog

This small fanged frog has interrupted dorsolateral folds, elongated dorsal ridges and incomplete toe webbing. The lower Mount Kitanglad comparison also notes relatively long hind limbs and narrower toe webbing than in L. magnus. AmphiBIO gives a compiled body-size value of 49.6 mm (Plaza et al., 2025; Beukema, 2011; Oliveira et al., 2017). The Philippine Swamp Frog has records from Mindanao, Samar, Bohol, and Dinagat, including Mount Hamiguitan Range on Mindanao and Samar Island Natural Park (GBIF.org, 2026; Maglangit et al., 2022; Supsup et al., 2017). At Tinuy-an Falls, this species was found on fallen logs, the forest floor, and stream margins. Taguibo Watershed records included terrestrial and vegetation and trees, while a northeastern Leyte inventory described observations in aquatic habitats (Plaza et al., 2025; Sanguila et al., 2021; Decena et al., 2023). At two lower Mount Kitanglad sites, frogs of several life stages were found under rocks by day, and night activity was noted at one site (Beukema, 2011). In the Hijo Forest survey, L. leytensis was the most frequently encountered frog species (Gamalo et al., 2023). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Other Wildlife Species (OWS).

*Account references:* Plaza et al., 2025; Beukema, 2011; Oliveira et al., 2017; GBIF.org, 2026; Maglangit et al., 2022; Supsup et al., 2017; Sanguila et al., 2021; Decena et al., 2023; Gamalo et al., 2023.

#### *Limnonectes magnus* · Mindanao Fanged Frog

The revised fanged-frog complex is differentiated using comparative characters and geographic locality (Abraham et al., 2021). The Tinuy-an account describes a large frog with a conspicuous tympanum, rounded snout and fully webbed feet (Plaza et al., 2025). The Mindanao Fanged Frog has records across Mindanao, Samar, Leyte, Bohol, Dinagat and Siargao. Mindanao localities include the Mount Hilong-Hilong, Mount Magdiwata, Mount Balatukan, Mount Lumot, Mount Hamiguitan and Mount Malindang ranges, as well as the Pasonanca area of the Zamboanga Peninsula. On Samar, records include Samar Island Natural Park and Mount Huraw; on Bohol, Rajah Sikatuna Protected Landscape (GBIF.org, 2026; Maglangit et al., 2022; Supsup et al., 2017; Nuñeza et al., 2010; Diesmos et al., 2026). A 2025 survey documented Mindanao Fanged Frogs along the Miral River in Bansalan, Davao del Sur (Ubat, 2025). The species also uses small, shallow forest streams and adjacent banks, ground surfaces and low vegetation. At Tinuy-an Falls Protected Landscape, individuals were observed in small, shallow forest streams and along their banks. Other Mindanao records include ground and vegetation close to forested riparian zones (Plaza et al., 2025; Sanguila et al., 2021). The Taguibo Watershed study reported this frog from ground, arboreal and aquatic habitats, drawing on both historical and recent records (Sanguila et al., 2021). In northeastern Leyte, the species was recorded in terrestrial and aquatic habitats (Decena et al., 2023). Stomach-content and stable-isotope analyses in a northeast Mindanao stream study supported generalist feeding on locally available invertebrate prey in the sampled Limnonectes magnus (Plaza et al., 2021). Compiled ecological profiles describe the species as terrestrial and aquatic, with larval development. The same compilation lists arthropods in its diet (Oliveira et al., 2017). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Other Threatened Species (OTS).

*Account references:* Abraham et al., 2021; Plaza et al., 2025; GBIF.org, 2026; Maglangit et al., 2022; Supsup et al., 2017; Nuñeza et al., 2010; Diesmos et al., 2026; Ubat, 2025; Sanguila et al., 2021; Decena et al., 2023; Plaza et al., 2021; Oliveira et al., 2017.

#### *Limnonectes micrixalus* · Western Mindanao Fanged Frog

AmphiBIO gives a compiled body-size value of 30 mm (Oliveira et al., 2017). The Abungabung Wart Frog was originally described from Abungabung on Basilan under the name Rana micrixalus. The current Amphibian Species of the World account lists both Basilan and mainland Mindanao, whereas Evans and colleagues (2003) treated it as restricted to Basilan. Its mainland occurrence therefore needs a traceable specimen-level reassessment before specific Mindanao localities are accepted (Evans et al., 2003; Frost, 2026). Compiled accounts describe the Western Mindanao Fanged Frog as terrestrial and aquatic, with a larval stage (Oliveira et al., 2017). Shaded stream margins and damp riparian ground may offer activity sites. Its listed Philippine DENR category is Other Wildlife Species (OWS).

*Account references:* Oliveira et al., 2017; Evans et al., 2003; Frost, 2026.

#### *Limnonectes parvus* · Philippine Guardian Frog

A male observed in Pasonanca Natural Park measured 30 mm snout–vent length and had smooth dorsal skin, a smooth supratympanic fold, first and second fingers of equal length, and an inverted V-shaped scapular mark (Lama & Senarillos, 2023). The Philippine Guardian Frog has been documented on Mindanao and Dinagat. On Mindanao, records include Mount Malindang Range, Pasonanca area, Mount Sinaka, Zamboanga del Norte, and Sarangani (GBIF.org, 2026). Records from Mindanao include secondary-growth and nursery forests with retained vegetation cover (Sanguila et al., 2016). Reported around streams in undisturbed lowland and lower montane forests of Mindanao (Frost, 2026). A male carrying tadpoles on its back was observed in Zamboanga (Lama & Senarillos, 2023). Compiled ecological profiles describe activity both on land and in water, with a larval developmental stage (Oliveira et al., 2017). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Other Wildlife Species (OWS).

*Account references:* Lama & Senarillos, 2023; GBIF.org, 2026; Sanguila et al., 2016; Frost, 2026; Oliveira et al., 2017.

#### *Limnonectes visayanus* · Visayan Fanged Frog

AmphiBIO gives a compiled body-size value of 94.2 mm (Oliveira et al., 2017). The Visayan Fanged Frog has records from Leyte and Bohol. On Leyte, records include Mount Pangasugan and Southern Leyte. On Bohol, records include Rajah Sikatuna Protected Landscape (GBIF.org, 2026; GIZ, 2013). Compiled accounts describe the Visayan Fanged Frog as nocturnal and terrestrial-aquatic, with larval development (Oliveira et al., 2017). Within its documented island range, saturated ground and shallow forest-stream margins may provide activity sites. Its listed Philippine DENR category is Vulnerable (VU).

#### *Occidozyga diminutiva* · Tiny Puddle Frog

The Tiny Puddle Frog is represented in a recent comparative diagnosis by very small adults, with a snout–vent length of about 18.0 mm in males and 26.4 mm in females in the cited historical material. The tongue is slightly notched behind and the lower jaw has a single tooth-like projection near its tip (Ninh et al., 2026, comparing Taylor, 1922). The Tiny Puddle Frog has been documented on Mindanao, including Pasonanca area, Zamboanga del Norte, and Zamboanga del Sur (GBIF.org, 2026; Frost, 2026). Compiled accounts place the Tiny Puddle Frog in terrestrial and aquatic environments, with a larval stage (Oliveira et al., 2017). Small puddles and shallow standing water may provide activity sites during wet periods. Its listed Philippine DENR category is Other Wildlife Species (OWS).

*Account references:* Ninh et al., 2026, comparing Taylor, 1922; GBIF.org, 2026; Frost, 2026; Oliveira et al., 2017.

#### *Occidozyga laevis* · Common Puddle Frog

AmphiBIO gives a compiled body-size value of 48 mm (Oliveira et al., 2017). The Common Puddle Frog has records from Mindanao, Samar, Leyte, Bohol, and Dinagat. On Mindanao, records include Cabadbaran–Santiago forest, Mount Balatukan, Pasonanca area, Mount Hilong-Hilong, Taguibo Watershed, Mount Lumot, and Agusan del Norte. On Samar, records include Samar Island Natural Park, Mount Huraw, and Eastern Samar (GBIF.org, 2026). On northeastern Leyte, Common Puddle Frogs have been recorded in aquatic habitats (Decena et al., 2023). Small pools, muddy margins and waterlogged ground along lowland tracks may provide suitable activity sites. A northeastern Mindanao stream-food-web study characterized the species as an opportunistic generalist predator on locally available insects, using stomach contents and stable isotope analysis (Plaza et al., 2021). At northeastern Leyte sites, frogs sat immersed in narrow shallow streams or isolated pools and sometimes sheltered beneath deep leaf litter by day (Decena et al., 2023). Compiled ecological profiles describe the species as fossorial and aquatic, with larval development. The same compilation lists arthropods in its diet, both diurnal and nocturnal activity and offspring counts of 107–140 (Oliveira et al., 2017). #### *Eleutherodactylus planirostris* · Greenhouse Frog

A small brown to grayish-brown frog with variable mottling and sometimes paired dorsolateral lines, measuring roughly 12–30 mm in body length (Animal Diversity Web, n.d.). The Greenhouse Frog is recorded on Mindanao and Bohol. On Mindanao, records include Mount Agad-Agad, Agusan del Norte, and Davao del Norte (GBIF.org, 2026; Maglangit et al., 2022). Greenhouse Frogs develop directly inside terrestrial eggs, with froglets hatching without an aquatic tadpole stage (Animal Diversity Web, n.d.). Damp leaf litter, planted beds and sheltered ground cover in gardens and settlements can provide terrestrial cover. Compiled ecological profiles describe the species as terrestrial, aquatic and arboreal, and indicate direct development without a free-living larval stage. The same compilation lists arthropods in its diet, nocturnal activity, and offspring counts of 2–26 (Oliveira et al., 2017). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Other Wildlife Species (OWS).

*Account references:* Animal Diversity Web, n.d.; GBIF.org, 2026; Maglangit et al., 2022; Oliveira et al., 2017.

### Reptiles

#### *Crocodylus mindorensis* · Philippine Crocodile

The Philippine Crocodile is a comparatively small freshwater crocodile with a broad head, prominent dorsal armour and a strong tail. Its dark-brown upperparts bear darker transverse bars above a paler underside. Enlarged postoccipital and nuchal scales help distinguish it from the Saltwater Crocodile; a comparative study recorded about 22–25 ventral scale rows and 4–6 enlarged postoccipital scales in examined Philippine Crocodiles (DENR Protected Areas and Wildlife Bureau / Royal Melbourne, 2005; Ross & Alcala, 1983, as reviewed in later crocodile literature). The Philippine Crocodile is documented in central Mindanao, including Ligawasan Marsh and the Muleta River system of Bukidnon (IUCN SSC and Secretariat, 2025). Philippine Crocodiles inhabit freshwater rivers, creeks, ponds, lakes and marshes, including quiet channels and vegetated margins with nearby basking and shelter sites. Published habitat accounts describe both riverine and wetland settings; northern Sierra Madre surveys in Luzon also reported animals in coastal river reaches with saline conditions (DENR Protected Areas and Wildlife Bureau / Royal Melbourne, 2005; Smithsonian National Zoo, n.d.; Brown et al., 2013). Females build mound or hole nests and both sexes may guard them. The Smithsonian account reports clutches of seven to thirty eggs (Smithsonian National Zoo, n.d.). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Critically Endangered (CR).

*Account references:* DENR Protected Areas and Wildlife Bureau / Royal Melbourne, 2005; Ross & Alcala, 1983, as reviewed in later crocodile literature; IUCN SSC and Secretariat, 2025; Smithsonian National Zoo, n.d.; Brown et al., 2013.

#### *Crocodylus porosus* · Saltwater Crocodile

In the comparative scale material examined, Saltwater Crocodiles had approximately 29–34 ventral scale rows and 0–2 enlarged postoccipitals, compared with 22–25 ventral rows and 4–6 enlarged postoccipitals in Philippine Crocodiles. Other morphological characters are also used for identification (Ross & Alcala, 1983, as reviewed in subsequent crocodile literature). The Saltwater Crocodile is recorded on Mindanao (GBIF.org, 2026). Saltwater Crocodiles use mangrove creeks, estuaries, tidal channels and coastal waters, and may travel far upstream into freshwater rivers and lakes. Females build mound nests near water, including sheltered banks along suitable river and estuarine systems (Carpenter & Niem, 2001). The Saltwater Crocodile is an egg-laying species (Uetz et al., 2026). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Critically Endangered (CR).

*Account references:* Ross & Alcala, 1983, as reviewed in subsequent crocodile literature; GBIF.org, 2026; Carpenter & Niem, 2001; Uetz et al., 2026.

#### *Bronchocela philippina* · Philippine Bloodsucker

The 2026 revision of Philippine Bronchocela diagnoses this Mindanao lineage by short, lanceolate nuchal spines, a nuchal crest of 9–12 spines separated from the dorsal crest, and narrow, strongly keeled lateral body scales. Its combination of crest and scale characters is more informative than a general green lizard appearance (Supsup et al., 2026). The Philippine Bloodsucker has been documented on Mindanao, including Mount Hilong-Hilong and Surigao del Norte (Tirona et al., 2026). Philippine Bloodsuckers occur in forest and shrubland, where sunlit branches, edge saplings and other shrub vegetation may serve as perches.

*Account references:* Supsup et al., 2026; Tirona et al., 2026.

#### *Draco bimaculatus* · Two-spotted Flying Lizard

The patagia are supported by five elongate ribs and the head bears a black spot on each side (Sanguila et al., 2026). The Two-spotted Flying Lizard has been documented on Mindanao, Samar, Leyte, Bohol, and Dinagat, including Mount Hamiguitan Range on Mindanao and Mount Huraw on Samar (GBIF.org, 2026; Maglangit et al., 2022; Supsup et al., 2017). At Agusan Marsh, Two-spotted Flying Lizards were observed on relatively low perches in peat-swamp and regenerating forest (Sanguila et al., 2026). Northeastern Mindanao records also describe individuals on trunks near forest margins and adjoining coconut groves (Sanguila et al., 2016). In northeastern Leyte, the species was recorded in vegetation and trees (Decena et al., 2023). A single individual at the northeastern Leyte study site was observed on a tree trunk about 4 m above ground by day in riparian vegetation within selectively logged dipterocarp forest (Decena et al., 2023). Females lay eggs (Uetz et al., 2026). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Other Wildlife Species (OWS).

*Account references:* Sanguila et al., 2026; GBIF.org, 2026; Maglangit et al., 2022; Supsup et al., 2017; Decena et al., 2023; Uetz et al., 2026; Sanguila et al., 2016.

#### *Draco cyanopterus* · Chartreuse-spotted Flying Lizard

The Mt. Hamiguitan account describes grey dorsal coloration with dark blotches and chartreuse-green patagia edged in dark brown or black in observed individuals (Supsup et al., 2017). The Chartreuse-spotted Flying Lizard is recorded on Mindanao and Dinagat. On Mindanao, records include Mount Hamiguitan Range, Mount Kitanglad Range, Pasonanca area, Taguibo Watershed, Mount Magdiwata, Mount Lumot, and Agusan del Norte (GBIF.org, 2026; Supsup et al., 2017; Beukema, 2011). Surveys in Taguibo Watershed recorded Chartreuse-spotted Flying Lizards on trees and other vegetation in terrestrial habitat (Sanguila et al., 2021). In northeastern Mindanao, individuals were also recorded on coconut palms and exposed trunks bordering forest (Sanguila et al., 2016). The species lays eggs (Uetz et al., 2026). At Mt. Hamiguitan, one female was observed burying four eggs in sand along a riverbank, providing a site-specific record of nesting behavior (Supsup et al., 2017). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Other Wildlife Species (OWS).

*Account references:* Supsup et al., 2017; GBIF.org, 2026; Beukema, 2011; Sanguila et al., 2021; Uetz et al., 2026; Sanguila et al., 2016.

#### *Draco guentheri* · Günther's Flying Lizard

A photographically identified Mt. Kitanglad individual displayed vivid malachite-green coloration and ultramarine-blue ciliary scales. The lizard was not captured for detailed examination (Beukema, 2011). The Günther's Flying Lizard has been documented on Mindanao, including Mount Kitanglad Range, Zamboanga del Sur, and Bukidnon (GBIF.org, 2026; Beukema, 2011). Fieldworkers recorded Günther's Flying Lizard in secondary-growth forest and on coconut trees beside the forest near Malagos, Mindanao (McGuire & Alcala, 2000). The species lays eggs (Uetz et al., 2026). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Other Wildlife Species (OWS).

*Account references:* Beukema, 2011; GBIF.org, 2026; Uetz et al., 2026; McGuire & Alcala, 2000.

#### *Draco mindanensis* · Mindanao Flying Lizard

A relatively large Philippine Draco with rib-supported gliding membranes. Males show a bright orange dewlap and red ventral patagia, whereas females have dark ventral patagia; body and membrane patterns distinguish it from sympatric flying lizards (Siler, n.d.). The Mindanao Flying Lizard is recorded on Mindanao, Samar, Leyte, and Dinagat. On Mindanao, records include Pasonanca area, Mount Magdiwata, and Agusan del Sur. On Samar, records include Samar Island Natural Park, Mount Huraw, and Eastern Samar. On Leyte, records include Southern Leyte (GBIF.org, 2026; GIZ, 2013). Mindanao Flying Lizards have been observed on tree trunks in primary and mature secondary rainforest; field accounts describe daytime activity several metres above the ground, commonly around 5–10 m (Sanguila et al., 2016). Like other Draco, it glides between trees using laterally extended, rib-supported patagial membranes (Siler, n.d.). This flying lizard lays eggs (Uetz et al., 2026). In Smith’s 1982 eastern Mindanao field series, a female from primary forest around 650 m elevation carried two oviductal eggs measuring 17.5 and 18.4 mm. Examined stomach contents included insects from several families (Smith, 1993). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Other Wildlife Species (OWS).

*Account references:* Siler, n.d.; GBIF.org, 2026; GIZ, 2013; Uetz et al., 2026; Smith, 1993; Sanguila et al., 2016.

#### *Draco ornatus* · Ornate Flying Lizard

The gliding membranes show conspicuous pattern differences between the sexes; females can be more vividly patterned than males (Uetz et al., 2026). The Ornate Flying Lizard has records from Mindanao, Samar, Leyte, Bohol, and Dinagat. On Mindanao, records include Andanan Watershed Forest Reserve and Bukidnon. On Samar, records include Mount Huraw and Eastern Samar (GBIF.org, 2026). Surveys have recorded this flying lizard on trees in primary and secondary forest and in coconut plantings close to forest; northeastern Leyte records include individuals using lowland coconut-plantation vegetation (Sanguila et al., 2016; Decena et al., 2023). The species lays eggs (Uetz et al., 2026). Four individuals of this flying lizard were seen on a single occasion in coconut-plantation vegetation in the lowlands of the Babatngon Range, Leyte (Decena et al., 2023). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Other Wildlife Species (OWS).

*Account references:* Uetz et al., 2026; GBIF.org, 2026; Decena et al., 2023; Sanguila et al., 2016.

#### *Draco reticulatus* · Reticulated Flying Lizard

The taxon historically described as Draco volans reticulatus has a dark ring around the eye, a white chin crossed by a fine network of dark spots, and pale patches at the sides of the nape. The male was described with an enlarged scale above the eye and a nuchal crest of more than 22 scales, while the lateral gliding membrane sometimes shows blue areas (The Reptile Database, 2026, after Musters 1983). The Reticulated Flying Lizard has been documented on Leyte and Bohol (GBIF.org, 2026). In the revision’s field observations, Draco reticulatus occurred mainly in open coconut plantations and was observed using well-sunlit trees on Bohol in sympatry with D. bimaculatus and D. ornatus. The species is part of the egg-laying Draco radiation, with oviparity reported for this taxon in the species treatment (Uetz et al., 2026). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Other Wildlife Species (OWS).

*Account references:* The Reptile Database, 2026, after Musters 1983; GBIF.org, 2026; Uetz et al., 2026.

#### *Gonocephalus semperi* · Anglehead Lizard

This lizard has a large head, strong hindlimbs, and a neck crest generally separated from the lower dorsal crest. The base of the tail shows alternating pale and dark bands; mature males commonly have blue eyes (Sanguila et al., 2026). Lizards reported as Gonocephalus semperi have been recorded on Mindanao, Samar, Leyte and Bohol. A recent taxonomic reassessment indicates that some older island records may belong to different lineages (Del Prado et al., 2026; recent Gonocephalus revision, 2026; GBIF.org, 2026). In Agusan Marsh, lizards identified as Gonocephalus semperi were encountered in Katigbok peat-swamp forest. They are active by day and have been observed sleeping head-up on sapling trunks near streams and rivers (Sanguila et al., 2026). The species lays eggs (Uetz et al., 2026). In the 1982 eastern Mindanao lizard collection, one female of the taxon then reported as Gonyocephalus semperi contained three developing eggs measuring 5.8, 6.6 and 7.3 mm. The same paper recorded insect and other arthropod remains in examined stomachs. This preserves the historical name and specimen scope (Smith, 1993). An archipelago-wide multilocus analysis recovered 12 putative Philippine Gonocephalus lineages, including differentiated southern island populations. Welton and colleagues identified several genetic lineages in this complex, with precise island assignments awaiting diagnosable specimen comparisons (Welton et al., 2017). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Other Threatened Species (OTS).

*Account references:* Sanguila et al., 2026; Del Prado et al., 2026; recent Gonocephalus revision, 2026; GBIF.org, 2026; Uetz et al., 2026; Smith, 1993; Welton et al., 2017.

#### *Hydrosaurus pustulatus* · Philippine Sailfin Lizard

The Philippine Sailfin Lizard is a large agamid with a distinctive laterally flattened, serrated dorsal sail extending from the hip region toward the base of the tail. The hind limbs are longer than the forelimbs, and the scales beneath the toes are conspicuously expanded. A juvenile from the Agusan River measured 95 mm snout–vent length (Sanguila et al., 2026). The Philippine Sailfin Lizard is recorded on Mindanao, Samar, Leyte, Bohol, Dinagat, and Siargao, including Mount Hilong-Hilong on Mindanao and Samar Island Natural Park (GBIF.org, 2026; Sanguila et al., 2021; Maglangit et al., 2022; Gamalo et al., 2023; Diesmos et al., 2026). Recorded on overhanging trees beside streams and riverbanks near Agusan Marsh agricultural land (Sanguila et al., 2026). The Taguibo study associated the sampled sailfin lizards with ground-level habitats in its riparian and terrestrial survey-zone analysis (Sanguila et al., 2021). In northeastern Leyte, the species was recorded in aquatic habitats (Decena et al., 2023). In northeastern Mindanao, sailfin lizards were observed active by day and resting in rock crevices along rivers at night at sampled sites between 170 and 420 m elevation (Sanguila et al., 2016). At Hijo Forest in Tagum City, observers found one lizard on a pandan plant and another crossing a coastal road near beach forest (Gamalo et al., 2023). In northeastern Leyte, individuals were observed sleeping on stream boulders or low shrubs at night and running across water in daylight (Decena et al., 2023). The species lays eggs (Uetz et al., 2026). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Other Threatened Species (OTS).

*Account references:* Sanguila et al., 2026; GBIF.org, 2026; Sanguila et al., 2021; Maglangit et al., 2022; Gamalo et al., 2023; Diesmos et al., 2026; Decena et al., 2023; Sanguila et al., 2016; Uetz et al., 2026.

#### *Ahaetulla prasina* · Asian Vine Snake

Green, grey, yellow and brown color forms are documented for this variable vine snake (Uetz et al., 2026). The Asian Vine Snake is recorded on Mindanao, Samar, and Leyte. On Mindanao, records include Mount Hamiguitan Range, Andanan Watershed Forest Reserve, Mount Hilong-Hilong, Taguibo Watershed, Mount Lumot, and Agusan del Norte. On Samar, records include Mount Huraw and Eastern Samar. On Leyte, records include Southern Leyte (GBIF.org, 2026; GIZ, 2013). Taguibo Watershed records place this vine snake in trees and other vegetation (Sanguila et al., 2021). Asian Vine Snakes have been seen sleeping on low shrubs beside forest edges (Sanguila et al., 2016). Slender branches, vines and shrub foliage offer climbing sites where their narrow green bodies blend into the vegetation. This diurnal, arboreal snake gives birth to live young (Uetz et al., 2026).

*Account references:* Uetz et al., 2026; GBIF.org, 2026; GIZ, 2013; Sanguila et al., 2021; Sanguila et al., 2016.

#### *Boiga angulata* · Philippine Blunt-headed Cat Snake

The Philippine Blunt-headed Cat Snake has a series of roughly forty dark crossbands on the back that broaden toward the sides. The illustrated Philippine snake key also distinguishes it from similar Boiga by nineteen rows of dorsal scales at midbody and a single preocular scale (Weinell et al., 2019). The Philippine Blunt-headed Cat Snake is recorded on Samar, Leyte, and Bohol. On Leyte, records include Southern Leyte (GBIF.org, 2026; GIZ, 2013). Comparative habitat accounts characterize the Philippine Blunt-headed Cat Snake as arboreal (Uetz et al., 2026). Intertwined branches and lower-canopy vegetation may offer places for nighttime activity. The species lays eggs (Uetz et al., 2026). A multilocus phylogeny placed Boiga angulata in the drapiezii species group alongside B. schultzei and other relatives. This phylogenetic placement provides no diagnostic field characters or Philippine island-level occurrence records by itself (Weinell et al., 2021). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Other Threatened Species (OTS).

*Account references:* Weinell et al., 2019; GBIF.org, 2026; GIZ, 2013; Uetz et al., 2026; Weinell et al., 2021.

#### *Boiga cynodon* · Dog-toothed Cat Snake

The Dog-toothed Cat Snake is distinguished in the illustrated Philippine key by twenty-three dorsal scale rows around midbody and three anterior temporal scales on each side of the head. These scale characters help separate it from similar Philippine Boiga (Weinell et al., 2019). The Dog-toothed Cat Snake has been documented on Mindanao, Leyte, Bohol, and Dinagat. On Mindanao, records include Mount Hamiguitan Range, Pasonanca area, Mount Agad-Agad, Lanao del Norte, and Davao Oriental (GBIF.org, 2026; Maglangit et al., 2022; Supsup et al., 2017; Sanguila et al., 2016). In the Leyte Cordillera and Babatngon ranges, the species was encountered in ground-level habitats (Decena et al., 2023). A phylogenetic study compared Boiga cynodon populations from the Philippines, Sundaland and Indochina (Weinell et al., 2021). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Other Threatened Species (OTS).

*Account references:* Weinell et al., 2019; GBIF.org, 2026; Maglangit et al., 2022; Supsup et al., 2017; Sanguila et al., 2016; Decena et al., 2023; Weinell et al., 2021.

#### *Boiga dendrophila* · Mangrove Snake

The Mindanao subspecies B. d. latifasciata has a black body with pale crossbands and vertical pupils (Plaza et al., 2025). The Mangrove Snake has records from Mindanao, Samar, Leyte, and Bohol. On Mindanao, records include Mount Hamiguitan Range, Pasonanca area, Bega Watershed, Agusan del Sur, and Davao Oriental. On Samar, records include Mount Huraw and Eastern Samar (GBIF.org, 2026; Supsup et al., 2017). Climbing and use of aboveground vegetation are documented; a comparative review characterizes this species as partly arboreal (Uetz et al., 2026). An adult Mangrove Snake was observed swimming through a shallow stream at Tinuy-an Falls (Plaza et al., 2025). Its climbing habits also fit creekside trees and mangrove branches above tidal water. The species lays eggs (Uetz et al., 2026). A multilocus analysis recovered several evolutionary lineages among members of the Boiga dendrophila complex, including B. d. melanota and B. tanahjampeana (Weinell et al., 2021). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Other Threatened Species (OTS).

*Account references:* Plaza et al., 2025; GBIF.org, 2026; Supsup et al., 2017; Uetz et al., 2026; Weinell et al., 2021.

#### *Boiga drapiezii* · White-spotted Cat Snake

Taylor’s diagnosis of this slender cat snake includes an enlarged vertebral scale row and a large preocular reaching the upper head shields, in addition to the two ventral black longitudinal lines described in the baseline (Uetz et al., 2026). The species lays eggs (Uetz et al., 2026). The White-spotted Cat Snake has records from Mindanao (Leviton et al., 2018). In shaded forest, slender branches and hanging vegetation may provide nighttime perches for the White-spotted Cat Snake. Its listed Philippine DENR category is Other Wildlife Species (OWS).

*Account references:* Uetz et al., 2026; Leviton et al., 2018.

#### *Calamaria gervaisii* · Philippine Reed Snake

This small reed snake has pale dorsal scales marked by dark networks and usually an interrupted pale stripe with dark borders along the lowest row of body scales. In the Philippine key, its chin shields contact the mental scale, and it lacks the dark belly bands of C. lumbricoidea (Weinell et al., 2019). The Philippine Reed Snake has records from Mindanao and Leyte. On Mindanao, records include Mount Hamiguitan Range, Mount Kitanglad Range, Mount Malindang Range, Mount Apo Natural Park, and Davao del Sur. On Leyte, records include Southern Leyte (GBIF.org, 2026; Beukema, 2011; GIZ, 2013). This species was recorded in soil and beneath forest-floor debris (Sanguila et al., 2016). An egg-laying snake (Uetz et al., 2026). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Other Wildlife Species (OWS).

*Account references:* Weinell et al., 2019; GBIF.org, 2026; Beukema, 2011; GIZ, 2013; Sanguila et al., 2016; Uetz et al., 2026.

#### *Calamaria lumbricoidea* · Variable Reed Snake

The Variable Reed Snake can be distinguished from several other Philippine Calamaria by dark bands across the underside of the body. Its mental scale also meets the front pair of chin shields, a useful feature in the illustrated identification key (Weinell et al., 2019). The Variable Reed Snake is recorded on Mindanao, Samar, and Bohol. On Mindanao, records include Mount Kitanglad Range, Andanan Watershed Forest Reserve, Mount Lumot, Misamis Oriental, and Bukidnon. On Samar, records include Samar Island Natural Park (GBIF.org, 2026; Beukema, 2011; Sanguila et al., 2016). In northeastern Leyte, the species was recorded in ground-level habitats (Decena et al., 2023). The snake lays eggs (Uetz et al., 2026). A single animal was encountered in daylight among leaf litter and riparian boulders within dipterocarp forest in northeastern Leyte. The study also discusses fossorial habits based on prior literature, so this paragraph reports only the directly documented daytime encounter (Decena et al., 2023). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Other Wildlife Species (OWS).

*Account references:* Weinell et al., 2019; GBIF.org, 2026; Beukema, 2011; Sanguila et al., 2016; Decena et al., 2023; Uetz et al., 2026.

#### *Chrysopelea paradisi* · Paradise Flying Snake

This slender snake has a dark back with reddish-yellow crossbars, a round pupil, and 17 dorsal scale rows at midbody. The outermost dorsal scale row is notched (Sanguila et al., 2026). The Paradise Flying Snake is recorded on Mindanao, Samar, and Leyte. On Mindanao, records include Pasonanca area, Davao del Norte, and Lanao del Norte. On Samar, records include Mount Huraw and Eastern Samar (GBIF.org, 2026; Gamalo et al., 2023). This snake is fully arboreal and uses the tree canopy and branches (Uetz et al., 2026). A tree-dwelling gliding snake, observed resting high in vegetation at night (Sanguila et al., 2016). Its lateral body shape allows controlled gliding between elevated positions; it reproduces by laying eggs (Uetz et al., 2026).

*Account references:* Sanguila et al., 2026; GBIF.org, 2026; Gamalo et al., 2023; Uetz et al., 2026; Sanguila et al., 2016.

#### *Coelognathus erythrurus* · Philippine Red-tailed Ratsnake

This rat snake has a round pupil and a brown-to-olive body; in the nominate form, the tail is noticeably paler or more reddish than the hindbody. Tail-color contrasts vary among the forms treated in the Philippine key and should be considered alongside other characters (Weinell et al., 2019; Sanguila et al., 2026). The Philippine Red-tailed Ratsnake has been documented on Mindanao, Samar, Leyte, Bohol, and Dinagat. On Mindanao, records include Andanan Watershed Forest Reserve, Misamis Occidental, and Lanao del Norte (GBIF.org, 2026; Sanguila et al., 2016). On northeastern Leyte, Philippine Red-tailed Ratsnakes were encountered in aquatic settings (Decena et al., 2023). Vegetated forest margins, fallen wood and ground cover may offer additional routes and retreats. The species lays eggs (Uetz et al., 2026). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Other Threatened Species (OTS).

*Account references:* Weinell et al., 2019; Sanguila et al., 2026; GBIF.org, 2026; Sanguila et al., 2016; Decena et al., 2023; Uetz et al., 2026.

#### *Dendrelaphis marenae* · Maren's Bronzeback Snake

A dark stripe extends behind the eye toward the neck, and a narrow pale stripe runs along the lower flanks; total length can reach about 131 cm (Uetz et al., 2026). This slender bronzeback has a broad dark postocular stripe extending onto the neck, a pale ventrolateral line and a particularly long tail comprising roughly one third of total length (Uetz et al., 2026). The Maren's Bronzeback Snake has been documented on Mindanao and Leyte. On Mindanao, records include Mount Hamiguitan Range, Taguibo Watershed, Agusan del Sur, and Davao Oriental. On Leyte, records include Southern Leyte (GBIF.org, 2026; Supsup et al., 2017; GIZ, 2013). Tree-dwelling Maren's Bronzeback Snakes have been recorded in the Agusan region (Sanguila et al., 2016). Sunlit shrubs and lower tree branches around forest openings may offer further activity sites. Two catalogued recent specimens from Taguibo Watershed (MBS 475 and MBS 481) were assigned to vegetation and trees in the field-record analysis (Sanguila et al., 2021). The species lays eggs (Uetz et al., 2026). Described as an active daytime snake that forages and moves among trees (Uetz et al., 2026). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Other Wildlife Species (OWS).

*Account references:* Uetz et al., 2026; GBIF.org, 2026; Supsup et al., 2017; GIZ, 2013; Sanguila et al., 2016; Sanguila et al., 2021.

#### *Dendrelaphis philippinensis* · Philippine Bronzeback Snake

Its midbody has thirteen dorsal scale rows, with dark longitudinal flank stripes and a pale lower-side stripe (Sanguila et al., 2026). Midbody dorsal scales number thirteen rows in examined specimens (Sanguila et al., 2026). The Philippine Bronzeback Snake has records from Mindanao, Samar, Leyte, and Bohol. On Mindanao, records include Andanan Watershed Forest Reserve, Pasonanca area, Mount Hilong-Hilong, Mount Agad-Agad, and Agusan del Norte. On Samar, records include Eastern Samar (GBIF.org, 2026; Maglangit et al., 2022). Philippine Bronzeback Snakes may move through shrubs and connected branches around forest gaps. Its listed Philippine DENR category is Other Wildlife Species (OWS).

*Account references:* Sanguila et al., 2026; GBIF.org, 2026; Maglangit et al., 2022.

#### *Dryophiops philippina* · Philippine Whip Snake

The Philippine Whip Snake is a slender, long-tailed snake that differs from the related D. rubescens in lacking the loreal scale between the nasal region and the eye. The illustrated key also reports fewer ventral scales in D. philippina, although the ranges approach one another (Weinell et al., 2019). The Philippine Whip Snake is recorded on Leyte and Mindanao. On Mindanao, records include Pasonanca area and Bukidnon (GBIF.org, 2026). The Philippine Whip Snake occurs in forest, where fine branches, hanging vines and dense shrubs may provide climbing routes. The original type locality of Dryophiops philippina is on northern Luzon. The Reptile Database documents its historical nomenclature and geographic records (Uetz et al., 2026). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Other Wildlife Species (OWS).

*Account references:* Weinell et al., 2019; GBIF.org, 2026; Uetz et al., 2026.

#### *Gonyosoma oxycephalum* · Red-tailed Green Ratsnake

A long, slender tree snake usually green or yellow-green over the body, with a tail that may be reddish, brown or gray. It has smooth dorsal scales; head-scale proportions and scale-row counts distinguish it from related Gonyosoma and allied genera. Tail coloration varies, and body pattern and scalation offer useful diagnostic characters (Wallach et al., 2024). The Red-tailed Green Ratsnake is recorded on Mindanao, Samar, Leyte, Bohol, and Dinagat. On Mindanao, records include Mount Hamiguitan Range, Pasonanca area, Zamboanga del Sur, and Davao Oriental. On Samar, records include Mount Huraw and Eastern Samar (GBIF.org, 2026; Supsup et al., 2017; Sanguila et al., 2016; Gamalo et al., 2023). Published accounts describe the Red-tailed Green Ratsnake as a diurnal tree-dwelling species (Uetz et al., 2026). Connected branches and climbing plants along forest margins may provide aboveground movement routes. This rat snake lays eggs (Uetz et al., 2026). In the Hijo inventory, this species was reported by site management but was neither documented in the authors’ recent fieldwork nor in the cited 2010 survey. The evidence is a local report, not a direct encounter (Gamalo et al., 2023). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Other Threatened Species (OTS).

*Account references:* Wallach et al., 2024; GBIF.org, 2026; Supsup et al., 2017; Sanguila et al., 2016; Gamalo et al., 2023; Uetz et al., 2026.

#### *Lycodon capucinus* · Common Wolf Snake

A slender, mostly brown or purplish-brown wolf snake with fine pale reticulations or broken light markings, sometimes accompanied by a pale collar behind the head. The color pattern can be faint, making scale characters and overall markings useful for identification (Lanza, 1999). The Common Wolf Snake is recorded on Mindanao, Samar, and Bohol. On Mindanao, records include Andanan Watershed Forest Reserve, Mount Hilong-Hilong, Mount Bato, Misamis Occidental, and Agusan del Norte. On Samar, records include Mount Huraw and Eastern Samar (GBIF.org, 2026; Gamalo et al., 2023). Common Wolf Snakes have been recorded near houses and in other modified lowland habitats (Sanguila et al., 2016), where sheltered ground and rock crevices may provide refuges. The Common Wolf Snake reproduces by laying eggs (Uetz et al., 2026). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Other Wildlife Species (OWS).

*Account references:* Lanza, 1999; GBIF.org, 2026; Gamalo et al., 2023; Sanguila et al., 2016; Uetz et al., 2026.

### Mammals

#### *Rusa marianna* · Philippine Brown Deer

PanTHERIA compiles adult mass 49457.6 g and head–body length 1255.0 mm for the historical species concept (Jones et al., 2009). The Philippine Brown Deer has been documented on Mindanao, Samar, and Leyte. On Mindanao, records include Mount Hamiguitan Range, Mount Kitanglad Range, Lake Lanao, and Sultan Kudarat. On Samar, records include Samar Island Natural Park. On Leyte, records include Mount Pangasugan and Southern Leyte (GBIF.org, 2026; GIZ, 2013). Philippine Brown Deer browse shoots, foliage and fruit in forest and along clearings, sometimes grazing adjoining grassland and moving through sheltered vegetation. Compiled traits also describe ground foraging and activity mainly at night and twilight (Ali et al., 2021; Wilman et al., 2014). The Mammal Diversity Database records the Mariana Islands as the original type locality, where the deer had been introduced (American Society of Mammalogists, 2026). The species was originally described as cervus Mariannus (A. G. Desmarest, 1822). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Endangered (EN).

*Account references:* Jones et al., 2009; GBIF.org, 2026; GIZ, 2013; Ali et al., 2021; Wilman et al., 2014; American Society of Mammalogists, 2026; A. G. Desmarest, 1822.

#### *Sus philippensis* · Philippine Warty Pig

The Greater Mindanao subspecies S. p. mindanensis is described with a mostly black crest and mane and a whitish crown tuft, differing from the forward-fringed head tuft of the Greater Luzon form (IUCN SSC Wild Pig Specialist Group, n.d.). The Philippine Warty Pig has been documented on Mindanao, Samar, and Leyte. On Mindanao, records include Mount Hamiguitan Range, Busa Mountain Range, Bega Watershed, Lake Lanao, and Davao del Norte. On Samar, records include Samar Island Natural Park. On Leyte, records include Mount Pangasugan (GBIF.org, 2026; Gamalo et al., 2023). Philippine Warty Pigs inhabit forest and adjoining vegetation, including montane and mossy forest in some areas. Rooted soil, wallows and disturbed leaf litter on the forest floor may indicate their activity. It eats fallen fruit, underground plant parts and invertebrates and may become more nocturnal in disturbed landscapes (IUCN SSC Wild Pig Specialist Group, n.d.; Wilman et al., 2014). The species was originally described as Sus philippensis (Nehring, 1886). Its historical type locality is Luzon, Philippines (American Society of Mammalogists, 2026). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Vulnerable (VU).

*Account references:* IUCN SSC Wild Pig Specialist Group, n.d.; GBIF.org, 2026; Gamalo et al., 2023; Wilman et al., 2014; Nehring, 1886; American Society of Mammalogists, 2026.

#### *Paradoxurus philippinensis* · Philippine Palm Civet

A shaggy greyish palm civet with a pale mask across the forehead, a small pale mark under each eye and pale spots beside the nostrils; the muzzle and ears are dark. The stocky body carries three rows of dark dorsal markings, while the lower legs and long tail are dark and largely unringed. WildAlert's external characters aid initial recognition of Philippine palm civets, with specialist traits used to distinguish related species (WildAlert, n.d.). The Philippine Palm Civet is recorded on Mindanao and Bohol. On Mindanao, records include Mount Kitanglad Range and Mount Apo (GBIF.org, 2026). Fruiting trees and connecting branches in woodland provide likely nighttime feeding and movement routes for the Philippine Palm Civet. The species was originally described as Paradoxurus philippinensis (Jourdan, 1837). Its historical type locality is Philippines (American Society of Mammalogists, 2026). Mammal Diversity Database v2.5 recognizes P. philippinensis following taxonomic separation from the P. hermaphroditus complex. Older Philippine records under P. hermaphroditus require taxon-concept reconciliation (American Society of Mammalogists, 2026).

*Account references:* WildAlert, n.d.; GBIF.org, 2026; Jourdan, 1837; American Society of Mammalogists, 2026.

#### *Viverra tangalunga* · Malay Civet

A series of black spots covers the greyish or tawny body; a dark stripe runs along the dorsal crest, and the tail carries contrasting pale and black bands. The feet are predominantly black (Animal Diversity Web, n.d.). The Malay Civet has been documented on Mindanao, Samar, Leyte, and Bohol. On Mindanao, records include Mount Hamiguitan Range, Mount Malindang Range, Mount Apo, and Bega Watershed. On Samar, records include Samar Island Natural Park. On Leyte, records include Southern Leyte (GBIF.org, 2026; GIZ, 2013). Malay Civets use forest, cultivated land and other modified terrestrial habitats, moving along ground-level routes through secondary woodland and vegetated edges. It is mainly nocturnal and ground-dwelling, occasionally climbing and sheltering in dense vegetation. The civet mainly moves and searches for food at night. It usually travels on the ground, but can climb trees, and shelters in dense vegetation during daylight (Animal Diversity Web, n.d.). EltonTraits estimates a diet of endothermic vertebrates (30%), ectothermic vertebrates (30%), invertebrates (10%), other plant material (10%), fruit (10%), other vertebrates (10%), with ground foraging and nocturnal, crepuscular and diurnal activity (Wilman et al., 2014). PanTHERIA compiles litter size 1.9 for comparison (Jones et al., 2009). The species was originally described as Viverra Tangalunga (J. E. Gray, 1832). Its historical type locality is West Sumatra (American Society of Mammalogists, 2026).

*Account references:* Animal Diversity Web, n.d.; GBIF.org, 2026; GIZ, 2013; Wilman et al., 2014; Jones et al., 2009; J. E. Gray, 1832; American Society of Mammalogists, 2026.

#### *Hipposideros antricola* · Philippine Dusky Roundleaf Bat

The original name Phyllorhina antricola was based on a male bat from an abandoned mine near Paracale, Luzon. Peters (1861) reported an ear about 17 mm long and 15 mm wide, a transverse noseleaf with four cells in his terminology, a conspicuous frontal sac in the male, and a tail about 30 mm long, nearly twice the approximately 16.5-mm tibia length. The original male had a forearm about 39 mm long; the wing membranes attached at the tarsus, the fur had pale bases and cinnamon-brown tips, and the wing membranes were brown. These are measurements and observations of the historical type specimen, not verified identification limits for all Philippine populations. Mammal Diversity Database recognizes H. antricola as distinct from the historical H. ater complex, with noseleaf and skull characters important in difficult identifications. Museum-grade comparison of noseleaf, cranial and dental characters is needed for difficult specimens; a flying bat or isolated forearm measurement is not enough. The Philippine Dusky Roundleaf Bat has been documented on Mindanao, including Cotabato (Hollister, 1913). The Philippine Dusky Roundleaf Bat is reported from forests, caves, and karst areas. The species was originally described as Phyllorhina antricola (W. C. H. Peters, 1861). Its historical type locality is Paracali, Luzon I, Philippines (American Society of Mammalogists, 2026). Mammal Diversity Database v2.5 recognizes this bat separately from the historical Hipposideros ater complex (American Society of Mammalogists, 2026).

*Account references:* Hollister, 1913; W. C. H. Peters, 1861; American Society of Mammalogists, 2026.

#### *Dyacopterus rickarti* · Philippine Large-headed Fruit Bat

The taxonomic revision treats this as a large Dyacopterus fruit bat: examined specimens had forearms exceeding approximately 90 mm and greatest cranial lengths exceeding approximately 40 mm, with palatal ridge counts also useful in distinguishing congeners. These were characters used in the original comparison, not standalone cutoffs for all populations (Helgen et al., 2007). The Philippine Large-headed Fruit Bat has been documented on Mindanao, including Mount Kitanglad Range (GBIF.org, 2026). Philippine Large-headed Fruit Bats may forage among fruiting trees and connected upper-canopy vegetation in forest. The species was originally described as Dyacopterus rickarti (K. M. Helgen, Kock, Gomez, & Ingle in K. M. Helgen, Kock, Gomez, Ingle, & M. H. Sinaga, 2007). Its historical type locality is "San Isidro (17°27'N, 120°37'E), Luzon (Abra Province), Philippines." (American Society of Mammalogists, 2026).

*Account references:* Helgen et al., 2007; GBIF.org, 2026; K. M. Helgen, Kock, Gomez, & Ingle in K. M. Helgen, Kock, Gomez, Ingle, & M. H. Sinaga, 2007; American Society of Mammalogists, 2026.

#### *Eonycteris robusta* · Philippine Dawn Bat

This comparatively large dawn bat has a long narrow muzzle. The species treatment reports forearm measurements of about 67–82 mm and distinguishes it from related taxa using cranial measurements (Wilson & Mittermeier, 2019). The Mindanao bat guide gives adult forearm length of 67–82 mm, measured from elbow to wrist (Ingle et al., n.d.). The Philippine Dawn Bat has been documented on Mindanao, Samar, Leyte, and Dinagat. On Mindanao, records include Awasian Water Forest Reserve, Mount Magdiwata, Zamboanga del Norte, and Misamis Oriental. On Samar, records include Mount Huraw and Eastern Samar. On Leyte, records include Mount Lobi Range (GBIF.org, 2026). The Philippine Dawn Bat feeds on nectar and can pollinate forest trees, linking its foraging ecology to flowering plants (Roño et al., 2021). Compiled traits describe nectar feeding, arboreal foraging and nocturnal activity (Wilman et al., 2014). The species was originally described as Eonycteris robusta (G. S. Miller, 1913). Its historical type locality is "cave at Montalban [Rizal Province], Luzon, Philippine Islands." (American Society of Mammalogists, 2026). Its listed Philippine DENR category is Vulnerable (VU).

*Account references:* Wilson & Mittermeier, 2019; Ingle et al., n.d.; GBIF.org, 2026; Roño et al., 2021; Wilman et al., 2014; G. S. Miller, 1913; American Society of Mammalogists, 2026.
